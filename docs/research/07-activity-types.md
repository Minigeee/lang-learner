# 07 · Activity types (grounded in SLA and learning science)

**Question:** What lesson and activity types does second-language-acquisition (SLA) research support for an adult Mandarin beginner who follows Refold (input before output)? And how should variety be generated, if not by doc 06's "neighbours never share an archetype" rule?
**Researched:** 2026-11-02. **Status:** a framework and candidate catalogue. **No matrix is final.** The choices are left to the user (see Open questions).

---

## TL;DR

- **Pick activities by what the lesson must train, not by rotation.** Practice transfers best when its processing matches the target skill. This is *transfer-appropriate processing* [29]. A tone lesson needs tone identification. A 了 lesson needs an activity where 了 decides the meaning. Doc 06's Vue archetypes were rhetorical shapes for explanations, not activities derived from a target skill.
- **Seven dimensions describe any activity:**
  1. target subskill;
  2. processing direction (bottom-up decoding vs top-down meaning-building);
  3. Nation strand;
  4. support level (gradual release);
  5. modality;
  6. output level (0 none / 1 light-scaffolded / 2 production);
  7. material novelty (seen vs unseen).
- **18 candidate activity types**, all at output level 0 or 1 (catalogue in §3). The strongest-evidenced for Phases 0–1:
  - **high-variability tone identification** [17][18][19][20];
  - **structured-input (referential) activities** from Processing Instruction [3][4];
  - **multi-pass listening** with a read-along pass [11][13][16];
  - **picture-supported CI / listen-and-do** [5][9];
  - **audio-first SRS retrieval** [23];
  - an **unseen-clip check** as the "you do".
- **Interleaving helps when the items are confusable.** Mixing 不/没 or 2nd/3rd tone beats blocking them, especially across sessions [24][25][26]. We found **no evidence that rotating activity *formats* improves learning.** Each new format costs working memory to learn, and novices pay the most [22][28]. Novelty mostly buys engagement, which fades after about 4 weeks [30].
- **Variety principle:** keep a **stable routine of formats per lesson shape**, and vary the content inside it:
  - voices, speakers, contexts, clips (high variability);
  - confusable items, interleaved and spaced;
  - support, faded across a unit.
  Check strand balance **per week**, not per lesson. Format novelty is an optional, capped motivation lever ("one wildcard per unit"), not a learning rule.
- **Lesson shapes:** the evidence supports **four shapes, chosen by idea type**: Sound, Pattern, Clip, Checkpoint. All four share doc 06's skeleton. The shape follows from the idea; it is not picked for variety.
- **Conflicts with docs 03/06:**
  - doc 06 puts shadowing in Phases 0–2 "we do", but Refold puts chorusing in 3D/4A [34];
  - S0 runs at about 100% language-focused learning, against Nation's ≤25% guideline [1];
  - the README says 42 lessons, but doc 03 lists about 138;
  - doc 06's "Build to spec" and "Fix it" formats are production/metalinguistic tasks.

## Findings

### 1. What the research says, by framework

**Nation's Four Strands.** A balanced course has four strands [1][2]:
- **meaning-focused input (MFI):** listening and reading, with the learner's attention on the message;
- **meaning-focused output (MFO);**
- **language-focused learning (LFL):** deliberate study of sounds, spelling, vocabulary and grammar;
- **fluency development (FD):** already-known material, under pressure to go faster, with no new language.

Nation and Yamamoto describe the strands as getting roughly equal time [2]. (reported: we couldn't open the 2007 PDF to quote the exact wording.) Two consequences matter here:
1. **LFL should not dominate.** About a quarter of the time is the guideline.
2. **FD is the strand most courses forget.** Refold's freeflow on already-studied content is FD under another name [34].

The genuine disagreement is MFO: Nation gives it a quarter, Refold gives it ~0% before Phase 4. That is a design choice the user has already made (input first); it is not a gap in the evidence.

**VanPatten's Processing Instruction (PI).** PI has three parts [3]:
- explicit information about the form;
- information about the processing strategy that misleads learners;
- **structured input**: activities where learners must process the form to get the meaning.

VanPatten and Sanz's guidelines are: present one thing at a time; keep meaning in focus; move from sentences to connected discourse; make the learner do something with every input sentence [3]. There are two activity kinds. **Referential** activities have one right answer, which depends on the form. **Affective** activities ask for an opinion or a "true for me" reaction [3]. (reported) The evidence: Shintani, Li & Ellis's meta-analysis of 35 comparisons found that comprehension-based and production-based grammar instruction both had large effects. Comprehension-based instruction was better on immediate receptive tests, and the edge came mostly from PI. Production-based instruction was better on *delayed productive* tests [4]. (verified, abstract) For an input-first learner, that is the trade-off stated plainly. PI-style input builds comprehension of a form well; production practice is what later makes the form usable in speech.

**Ellis / Shintani on input-based tasks.** Ellis notes that tasks can be input-based as well as output-based [5]. In Shintani's study, beginners did "listen-and-do" tasks. They acquired vocabulary and plural -s, *receptively and productively*, and beat a production-based PPP group on several measures. They were active even though no speech was required [5]. (verified, review) Caveats: it was one small study, with 6-year-olds.

**Schmidt's noticing hypothesis; enhancement and flood.** Schmidt argued that a form must be *noticed* in the input before it can be acquired [6]. (reported) The two classroom tools are:
- **input flood:** many tokens of the form;
- **visual input enhancement:** highlighting the form.

Lee & Huang's meta-analysis found only a small benefit of enhancement for grammar, and a small *cost* to comprehension of meaning [7]. (reported, figures not confirmed) **Implication:** highlight a pattern on a second pass, never on the first viewing of a clip.

**Narrow input.** Krashen proposed narrow listening: many short recordings on one topic, or of one speaker, so that vocabulary and context recur [8]. Dupuy's study of French learners is mostly learner self-report: they found it helpful and more interesting [8]. (reported) The evidence is weak but plausible, and the cost is low.

**CI teaching techniques.**
- **TPR** (acting out commands): Asher's own experiments found acting beat translating for retention [9]. Independent replications are thin. (reported)
- **TPRS** (storytelling with "circling", many easy questions about one sentence): Lichtman finds TPRS students ahead on some measures and level on listening and writing; critics call the evidence thin [10]. (reported)
- **Dreaming Spanish-style pure CI**: a product model with community evidence only [36]. We found no "Dreaming Chinese"; the Mandarin analogues are Mandarin Click, Lazy Chinese, Sensoba (doc 03).

No technique beats the others. What they share is understood input, many repetitions, and visual support.

**Reading-while-listening (RWL).** Chang and colleagues found that RWL and repeated listening improved listening fluency. Learners kept up with faster speech and with unpractised texts without losing comprehension [11]. (reported) The counter-evidence: Diao & Sweller found that adding audio to EFL *reading* comprehension caused a redundancy effect that hurt it [12]. (verified, citation and framing) The way these fit together, as an inference: RWL helps when the *goal is listening* and the text is easy. It hurts when the text is hard and the goal is reading. Captions research agrees: Montero Perez et al.'s meta-analysis found large effects of captions on L2 listening comprehension and vocabulary [13]. (verified, abstract)

**Intensive vs extensive listening.** Renandya & Farrell argue that extensive listening (lots of easy listening) is the neglected half, and that it builds automatic processing [14]. (verified) Field's critique of the usual "comprehension approach" is that it *tests* listening instead of teaching it. He separates **decoding** (sounds → words → literal meaning) from **meaning-building**. He argues that decoding training is the most effective way to improve a *novice* listener. He prescribes short "micro-listening" exercises after the main listening: partial dictation of a sentence played several times, word-boundary tasks, odd-one-out with similar-sounding words [15]. (reported, via book chapter titles and summaries)

**Metacognitive multi-pass listening.** Vandergrift's pedagogical cycle runs: predict → first listen → verify with a partner → second listen → final listen → reflect. In a controlled study it beat the same texts practised without guidance. **Less-skilled listeners gained the most** [16]. (verified, abstract) This is Refold's "Multipass" (1C) [34], with evidence behind it.

**High-variability phonetic training (HVPT) for tones.** HVPT has three defining features [19]:
- many talkers;
- many phonetic contexts;
- feedback on every trial.

The Mandarin evidence:
- Wang et al. trained Americans on Mandarin tones in eight sessions over two weeks. Accuracy rose from 69% to 90%, and the gain was retained after six months [17]. (verified, abstract)
- A follow-up found that tone *production* improved too, with no production training [18]. (verified, abstract) **This is the strongest single argument for Refold's "ears first" ordering.**
- Meta-analyses confirm HVPT's effect on perception [20]. Multiple talkers give a small but real advantage for generalising to new voices [20]. (reported)

Repetition is a positive factor across [8][11][16], but no source supports a specific number of passes.

### 2. What the learning science says

**Gradual release of responsibility.** Pearson & Gallagher's model, operationalized by Fisher & Frey as "I do / we do / you do together / you do alone" [21]. (reported) A solo app drops "together", leaving doc 06's three steps.

**Worked examples, fading, expertise reversal.** Novices learn more from worked examples than from solving problems unaided. The advantage reverses as expertise grows, and *fading* (removing steps gradually) beats both fixed approaches [22]. (reported) The language version of a worked example is a modelled, annotated native clip. Fading means fewer replays, fewer hints, less pinyin. **Fade across a unit, not only within a lesson.**

**Retrieval practice.** In Karpicke & Roediger's study, repeated retrieval of Swahili-English pairs produced large gains on a delayed test. Repeated restudy produced none, and students couldn't tell the difference [23]. (verified) Every "you do" and every SRS card is retrieval.

**Interleaving and contextual interference.** Brunmair & Richter's meta-analysis found a moderate interleaving effect (g = 0.42). It was **larger when categories are similar to each other** and smaller for expository text [24]. (verified, abstract) The L2 evidence:
- Nakata & Suzuki found that mixing grammar exercises improved long-term retention [25]. (reported)
- Pan et al. found that interleaving Spanish tenses helped **only when spread across sessions**. Within a single session, blocking did as well or better [26]. (reported)

**Crucially, all of this interleaves *content categories* (confusable items), not activity formats.** We found no study showing that switching between activity *types* improves learning. Transfer-appropriate processing points the other way: the format should match the target skill [29].

**Desirable difficulties.** Spacing, interleaving, retrieval and varied conditions slow practice down but improve retention [27]. (reported) They are desirable *only if the learner can succeed*. For a beginner, an unfamiliar format is an undesirable difficulty.

**Cognitive load.** Novices have little spare working memory. Redundant streams (the same words as audio *and* text, when one stream is enough) and unfamiliar task mechanics both cost capacity [12][28]. (reported) **A stable routine frees capacity for the language.**

**Dual coding / multimedia.** Verbal plus pictorial information is remembered better than either alone [28][37]. (reported) Picture-supported video is dual coding. Same-language text plus audio is *not* dual coding: both are verbal. It is the redundancy case above, and it helps listening only when the text is easy enough [11][12].

**Novelty vs predictability.** In a 14-week study, gamification's effect fell after about 4 weeks (novelty), then partly recovered (familiarization) [30]. (reported) Habit formation favours a stable routine (doc 04). Novelty is a weak, short-lived lever; predictability suits bedtime phone sessions.

### 3. Output timing

- **Refold:** almost no output before Phase 4. Chorusing first appears in 3D, and daily output (chorusing, reading aloud) in 4A. Its reason is perceptual: speaking before you can hear the distinctions may cement substitutes [34]. (verified, doc 01)
- **Swain:** output pushes learners to *notice* gaps, test hypotheses and reflect on the language [31]. (reported) These functions mostly need interaction or feedback, which a solo app at Phase 1 can't give well.
- **Early forced output:** Postovsky's delayed-oral-practice study (a four-week delay vs none, in Russian) is the classic test. We could confirm only its design, not its results [32]. The modern evidence is indirect:
  - comprehension-based instruction matches production-based instruction except on delayed production [4];
  - perception training improves production [18].
  Nothing we found shows that early output *harms* adults. Nothing shows it is *needed* for comprehension either.
- **Which light output is defensible early:**
  - **SRS recall** is retrieval. It may be silent (output 0) or said aloud (1).
  - **Single-word or gesture answers** (TPR/TPRS-style) keep the input flowing.
  - **Shadowing:** Hamada found that it improves phoneme perception and listening, especially for weaker learners. That is a *bottom-up perception* benefit, not a speaking one [33]. (reported)

  Shadowing therefore has a perception rationale even inside an input-first plan. It conflicts with Refold only in *timing*.

**Output levels used below:**
- **0 none:** attend, tap, choose, act, or type what you heard. Transcription counts as receptive.
- **1 light-scaffolded:** produce a model you have just heard or been cued with. Shadowing, chorusing, saying a single cued word, recalling a card aloud.
- **2 production:** generate language from meaning without a model. Excluded in Phases 0–2.

### (c) A principled way to generate variety

**Candid answer first.** For *learning*, the evidence favours:
- variability of **content**: talkers [17][20], contexts, clips;
- **interleaving confusable items** across sessions [24][26];
- spacing and retrieval [23][27];
- matching format to target skill [29].

It does **not** favour rotating activity formats. Unfamiliar formats add cognitive load for novices [22][28], and the gain from novelty fades within weeks [30]. Format variety is a *motivation* lever, worth something for a solo learner, but small and short-lived. Every input-first product we looked at runs one or two formats and varies only content (§4).

**Proposed rules** (replacing "neighbours never share an archetype"):

1. **Format follows target subskill.** The lesson's idea type picks the shape, and the shape fixes the default activities. Mapping:

   | Idea type | Shape | Default activities |
   |---|---|---|
   | sound contrast or sound rule | Sound | T1, T2, T7 |
   | form-meaning pattern or confusable pair | Pattern | T5, T6, T7, T8 |
   | vocabulary topic, story or clip | Clip | T10/T11, T9, T3, T4, T12 |
   | consolidation | Checkpoint | T14, T1 with new voices, T7 interleaved, T13 |

2. **Vary content, not form.** Use ≥4 voices per contrast. Never reuse the same speaker for the "you do" as for the "I do". Rotate contexts (kids' show / vlog / dialogue). The "you do" is always unseen material.
3. **Interleave confusables, spaced.** After two members of a confusable set have been taught (2nd/3rd tone, 不/没, 在/有, 二/两), later lessons' "we do" and the SRS mix them. This applies across sessions, not within the first lesson that introduces a form [26].
4. **Fade support across a unit.** Lesson 1 of a unit uses full pinyin, unlimited replays, and pre-glossed words. The last lesson uses fewer replays, hides pinyin by default, and ends with T14 on a new clip [22].
5. **Balance strands per week, measured, not per lesson.** Log minutes per strand, alongside Refold's categories. Targets: Refold's split per sub-phase (doc 01), plus two checks Nation adds:
   - LFL should not exceed roughly half in 1A–1B, or a quarter from 1C;
   - FD should be non-zero from 1A, via T15 on fully understood clips.
6. **Novelty is capped and optional.** At most one "wildcard" activity per unit (e.g. T13 narrow set, T9 listen-and-do), drawn from the phase's allowed set. Test it on the user: if completion rates or self-reported enjoyment drop on routine weeks, raise the cap.

### (d) Lesson shapes

The research supports **a stable routine per idea type**, not a free rotation of archetypes:
- the listening literature describes a *cycle* (predict → listen → verify → micro-listen) [15][16];
- PI describes a *sequence* (explicit info → referential → affective) [3];
- HVPT describes a *drill* with feedback [19].

These differ because their target subskills differ. All four shapes fit doc 06's skeleton (Hear it → ≤300-word explanation → we do → you do → cards):

| Shape | Hear it (I do) | Explanation | We do | You do | ~Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sound** | Native model across 2 voices | ≤150 words: what to listen for | T1 (10–20 trials, feedback), T2 for rules | T1 with 2 unseen voices, ≥80% | 5–6 min |
| **Pattern** | Clip containing the form (gist only) | ≤200 words + the misleading strategy (PI) | T5 (6–10 items) → T6 (2–3) | T5 on new sentences, or T3 on a new clip | 6–7 min |
| **Clip** | T11 pass 1 (predict + gist), or T10 | ≤150 words on 2–3 key words | T11 pass 2 + T4 on decoding failures, or T9 | T11 pass 3 + gist check, or T14 | 6–7 min |
| **Checkpoint** | none | none | none | T14 ×2, T1 new voices, T7 interleaved | 7 min |

Doc 06's Mystery, Showdown and Walkthrough can survive as **ways of writing the explanation** (e.g. "Why does 你好 sound like *ní hǎo*?"). They are not activity types, and they shouldn't drive a variety rule.

## Open questions

1. **Shadowing before Phase 3?** Hamada's perception evidence [33] supports it; Refold's timing [34] doesn't. Options: (a) none until 3D; (b) optional and perception-gated from 1B (recommended); (c) in every "we do". Microphone recording is a separate v1 question (doc 06).
2. **How much format routine can the user tolerate?** The evidence says a stable routine is fine for learning. Only the user knows when boredom sets in. Proposal: start with the four fixed shapes and a wildcard cap of 1 per unit, then review after 4 weeks [30].
3. **Accept Nation's missing MFO strand?** Refold's choice means no meaningful output for ~1,500 h. Light level-1 activities are not MFO. Is that acceptable, given the meta-analysis finding that production practice wins on delayed productive tests [4]?
4. **Is affective structured input (T6) worth building** for a solo learner? It has weak evidence and no partner.
5. **Is listen-and-do on a phone (T9) real TPR?** Tapping is a weaker physical response than acting. Is it worth the picture-asset cost?
6. **First-clip subtitles in Clip lessons:** none, Chinese, or English gloss for 1A–1B? Captions help comprehension [13]. Field and Vandergrift want a first listen that trains listening [15][16].
7. **Unverified figures:** Nation's exact balance wording [1], Lee & Huang's effect sizes [7], Postovsky's results [32], Hamada's sample [33]. Check them before this doc is cited elsewhere.
